# Harmony

**120,000 SF Data Center | 2026 ASCE Sustainable Solutions Competition**  
**South Regional Runner-Up**

## Project Overview

Harmony was developed for the 2026 ASCE Sustainable Solutions Competition, which challenged teams to design a sustainable 120,000-square-foot data center. As the lead designer for Vanderbilt’s team, I developed the project around a central question: **how can one of the most resource-intensive building types become an extension of its surrounding environment rather than an interruption of it?**

Data centers are typically associated with large, highly industrial buildings, significant energy consumption, and substantial cooling demands. Rather than attempting to hide these challenges, Harmony approaches them through the relationship between architecture, landscape, and infrastructure. The project uses vegetation, site planning, passive environmental strategies, and a deliberately restrained architectural language to create a data center that integrates into its landscape.

The name *Harmony* reflects the project's central ambition: creating a balance between technology and nature.

## Architecture as Landscape

The primary campus is organized around a circular geometry derived from the shape of a peace symbol. This geometry establishes the organizational framework for the site while creating a strong symbolic connection to the project's broader concept of coexistence.

Four primary data center volumes surround a central courtyard, creating a campus rather than a single monolithic structure. The separation between buildings introduces natural circulation through the site and creates a sequence of exterior spaces between the otherwise massive programmatic requirements of the data center.

This organization also reduces the perceived scale of the facility. Instead of presenting a single uninterrupted facade, the building mass is divided into smaller volumes that frame views, define outdoor spaces, and allow the landscape to become an active component of the architecture.

The central axis extends from the site entrance through the courtyard, creating a clear pedestrian sequence into the heart of the campus. The resulting space functions as both a circulation path and a moment of pause within an otherwise highly infrastructural environment.

## The Living Envelope

One of the defining features of Harmony is the use of living walls across the exterior of the data center buildings. Rather than treating vegetation as decorative landscaping placed around the building, the project incorporates it directly into the building envelope.

The vegetated facades help visually soften the scale of the data center while establishing a continuous relationship between the architecture and surrounding landscape. The buildings become partially concealed within their own vegetation, challenging the conventional image of a data center as a purely industrial object.

The living walls also reinforce the project's environmental strategy. By introducing vegetation directly onto the building envelope, the project explores how architectural surfaces can contribute to passive environmental performance while simultaneously improving the visual character of the site.

The intent was not simply to make a data center *look green*, but to make the landscape an integral part of how the building is experienced.

## Preserving the Existing Landscape

Site planning became another major component of the sustainability strategy. Rather than clearing the site to accommodate the new development, the existing orchard is preserved and incorporated into the campus.

The orchard serves multiple purposes. It maintains an existing ecological and visual feature while also acting as a natural screen between the data center and surrounding public areas. This allows the facility to maintain a lower visual presence from the surrounding roads while preserving the character of the existing landscape.

The landscape therefore becomes a form of infrastructure in its own right. Instead of separating the building from its surroundings, the orchard, living walls, circulation paths, and open spaces work together to create a continuous transition between developed and natural areas.

## Cooling and Environmental Strategy

Because cooling represents one of the major environmental challenges associated with data centers, the project explored strategies for reducing the building's dependence on conventional mechanical systems.

The organization of the campus creates opportunities for air movement between the individual data center volumes, while the extensive vegetation introduces additional layers of environmental buffering. The project also incorporates passive cooling strategies into the overall site and building approach rather than treating mechanical systems as the only solution to thermal performance.

Stormwater management is similarly integrated into the site plan through detention ponds positioned around the perimeter of the property. These systems allow the landscape to participate in managing the environmental impact of the development rather than relying entirely on conventional infrastructure.

Together, these strategies reflect the larger philosophy of Harmony: **sustainability should be embedded within the architecture and site from the beginning rather than added as a collection of independent technologies.**

## Infrastructure and Identity

A major challenge of the project was balancing the highly technical requirements of a data center with the desire to create an architecture that felt connected to its surroundings.

The substation and other necessary infrastructure are positioned within the larger site strategy rather than treated as separate objects. The existing orchard helps screen these elements, while the larger campus geometry organizes the relationship between technical infrastructure, buildings, and landscape.

The result is an attempt to rethink the identity of the data center. Instead of concealing the infrastructure completely, Harmony gives it a new architectural context—one in which technology exists within a carefully constructed landscape.

## Designing at Multiple Scales

Harmony required thinking simultaneously at the scale of the individual building, the campus, and the larger site.

At the building scale, the project explored the relationship between solid data center volumes and living facades. At the campus scale, the four-building arrangement establishes a central courtyard and creates a legible circulation system. At the site scale, the orchard, detention ponds, roads, pedestrian paths, and existing landscape work together to reduce the visual and environmental impact of the development.

This layered approach was particularly important to my role as lead designer. The project required balancing architectural form with site planning, environmental considerations, infrastructure, and the practical requirements of a large-scale data center.

## A Different Model for Data Centers

Harmony ultimately proposes that sustainability and technological infrastructure do not have to exist in opposition.

The project uses the landscape not as a backdrop for the building, but as an active architectural system. Vegetated walls soften the building envelope, the preserved orchard screens infrastructure, the courtyard creates a social center, and the site geometry establishes a clear relationship between the individual buildings.

The project asks whether the future of data center design can move beyond simply making industrial infrastructure more efficient and instead consider how that infrastructure can **participate in the landscape around it**.

Harmony is therefore less about hiding the data center and more about changing the way it belongs to its site—creating a future in which technology and nature can exist not only side by side, but in balance.
